    Lessons¶

    Hard-won notes on running TgTRG — behavior that is correct by implementation but easy to misread, and the practical consequences for how a run should be set up and judged. To be updated over time.

    How Large a Step May Be¶

    The step size is the one parameter of TgTRG whose effect is nothing like its effect in TDVP, and the reason is that imaginary-time propagators are not unitary. A real-time step cannot change the scale of anything; an imaginary-time one spreads the local spectrum over \(e^{\tau \Delta E}\) in magnitude, where \(\Delta E\) is the range of the effective Hamiltonian being exponentiated. Everything below follows from that single exponent.

    Not the only accuracy knob¶

    Start with the case where the step size does nothing at all. When the bond dimension is large enough that the manifold is the whole space, the tangent projector is the identity and the projector splitting integrates the flow exactly — at either order, and for any \(\tau\). Verified to 1e-10 on an L = 4 chain in a single tau = 8 step, and the test test_full_rank_step_is_exact keeps it honest.

    So the exponential grid is not making an approximation when it takes \(\tau = \beta\). What limits the step is arithmetic, and it comes in two regimes with very different manners of failing.

    Regime 1: the column stops being representable¶

    Inside lanczos_expv the propagated vector is the first column of \(e^{-\tau T}\), assembled as a sum over the eigenvectors of the projected matrix with weights \(e^{-\tau \lambda_j} \cdot \text{overlap}_j\). Those weights span \(e^{\tau \Delta E}\), and float64 holds exponents to about \(\pm 709\), so the sum stops being representable once \(\tau \Delta E\) passes roughly \(1400\).

    Probing one real step on the L = 4 chain, whose local spectrum runs over \([-2.24, +2.24]\):

    τ τ·ΔE smallest / largest naive column norm columns lost
    0.5 2 6.3e-1 / 1.5e0 none
    8 36 1.3e-4 / 7.7e3 none
    32 143 2.9e-16 / 3.5e15 none
    128 572 7.1e-63 / 1.4e62 none
    320 1431 4.2e-156 / inf 13 of 18 non-finite
    512 2290 0 / inf 4 zero, 13 non-finite

    This regime is closed off by construction: _propagator_coeffs assembles the column in log space and divides out its largest term, so the result is always a unit vector and the magnitude lives in log_scale. See Why the scale is measured every call for why that maximum is taken per call and not precomputed.

    Regime 2: the exponent exceeds what float64 can resolve¶

    The regime that remains open is forty times earlier in \(\tau\) and far less polite, because the run completes and reports plausible numbers. The backward bond update propagates by \(e^{+\tau H^{(0)}}\), amplifying the high-energy end of the local spectrum; once that amplification exceeds the inverse of float64 resolution, what it amplifies is roundoff.

    Measured on L = 4 free fermions, varying the hopping so that \(\Delta E\) changes while nothing else does. Note that order=2 propagates in half-steps of \(\tau/2\), so the exponent of a single propagation is \((\tau/2)\,\Delta E\):

    t ΔE τ (τ/2)·ΔE relative error on log Z
    1 4.5 0.5 1.1 4.0e-14
    1 4.5 8 17.9 4.1e-14
    2 8.9 8 35.8 2.0e-14
    1 4.5 32 71.6 9.0e-3
    4 17.9 8 71.6 unphysical: the run aborts

    The two failures sit at the same exponent, reached by different routes, and the safe cases are at or under \(36 \approx \log(1/\varepsilon)\) for float64. That is the criterion:

    \[ \frac{\tau}{2}\, \Delta E \lesssim \log(1/\varepsilon) \approx 36 \]

    for order=2, and \(\tau \Delta E \lesssim 36\) for order=1. The limit is on the exponent, not on \(\tau\) and not on \(\beta\): a chain with a wider spectrum reaches it at a proportionally smaller step.

    Nothing downstream notices. The Krylov expansions converge to 1e-13 in the failing step exactly as in the good ones, because they solve the local problem they were handed correctly — what drifts is the state they are applied to. The 1e-13 is not a reassurance.

    What the warning does and does not cover¶

    run() warns when a schedule contains a step past _TAU_SOFT_LIMIT = 8.0. That constant corresponds to \(\Delta E\) of order a few, which covers the free-fermion and Hubbard chains in the tests and examples, and it is deliberately conservative there. It is not universal: \(\Delta E\) grows with the chain length and with the coupling strengths, so on a long chain or a strongly interacting model the criterion above is reached at a step the warning will not mention. Read the criterion, not the constant, and treat the warning as a floor on caution, never a bound on it.

    The warning is a warning and not a refusal for the same reason: the driver does not know the spectral range, and a model normalized differently may be perfectly fine past the constant.

    Hence the stacked schedule¶

    The exponential grid walks into regime 2 unaided, its step size being \(\beta\). So the schedule to use is the stacked one — exponential out of the high-temperature regime where the Taylor state lives, linear from there on, as in the overview and both examples. The default n_steps of 13 ends the exponential phase at \(\beta = 2\), where that handover belongs for a spectrum of order a few.

    Step Size Versus Bond Dimension¶

    Under a rank limit the step size does buy accuracy, and not much of it. It is no substitute for the bond dimension.

    Two error sources are at work, and they scale differently in the number of steps. The splitting error is per step and shrinks with \(\tau\) in the usual way. The projection error is a rate: at every instant the exact flow \(-H\rho\) points partly out of the tangent space, and the component that cannot be followed accrues per unit of \(\beta\), not per step. Taking more steps over the same interval samples the same inability more finely without removing any of it.

    Cooling \(\beta = 0.5 \to 1.0\) at max_bond = 4, varying only how many steps cover the interval:

    steps τ order 1 order 2
    1 0.5 3.02e-3 2.23e-3
    2 0.25 2.23e-3 1.78e-3
    4 0.125 1.78e-3 1.55e-3
    8 0.0625 1.55e-3 1.44e-3
    16 0.031 1.44e-3 1.39e-3
    64 0.0078 1.36e-3 1.35e-3

    Sixty-four times the work buys a factor of 2.2, nearly all of it in the first three refinements, and the floor of 1.35e-3 is the manifold's. For scale, the state handed in at \(\beta = 0.5\) already carried 1.2e-4 from its own compaction to rank 4, and the best a rank-4 MPO can do at \(\beta = 1.0\) is 3.5e-3 — the tangent trajectory beats the pointwise truncation of the exact \(\rho\), which is worth knowing but not something to rely on.

    The order columns make the point from another angle. order=2 is a forward half-sweep of \(\tau/2\) and a backward one, so order 2 at n steps and order 1 at 2n steps perform the same number of half-sweeps at the same size — and they agree to every digit shown, diagonal by diagonal. In this regime the integrator order earns nothing, because what is being measured is not a discretization error at all.

    So tune in one order. Set max_bond from the accuracy you need, judged on Summary.discarded_weights; then take \(\tau\) as large as the exponent criterion allows, the sweeps it saves costing a fraction of what the rank already concedes. A refinement of \(\tau\) is worth one or two attempts after that, and almost nothing past them.

    Why the Scale Is Measured Every Call¶

    _propagator_coeffs recomputes its log-space shift on every call, which invites the question of whether one estimate of \(\lVert H \rVert\), taken once before the run, could serve instead. It cannot, and it would save nothing.

    The first thing to see is that the shift does not affect the answer. The routine forms exp(logs - c), assembles, normalizes, and folds c back into log_scale; for any c the returned coefficients and log_scale are identical. The shift's only job is to keep the intermediate exponentials in range, so the question is not whether a global constant is accurate but whether it can keep them there.

    It cannot, for two reasons. The exponents are not a property of \(H\) alone: each term carries \(-\tau \lambda_j + \log|\text{overlap}_j|\), and the overlap is between the local starting vector and the eigenvectors of the projected matrix — that is the state, and it changes at every site, every half-sweep, and every step. A shift drawn from the spectrum alone misses it, and when the propagator's favored eigenvector has little weight in the starting vector, every surviving coefficient is small and the column underflows to zero together. And on the spectrum itself a global \(\lVert H \rVert\) is only an upper bound — the local effective Hamiltonians are compressions of \(H\) by the isometries of the canonical form, so their eigenvalues lie inside \(H\)'s range but need not fill it. A bound loose by more than about 700 in the exponent underflows the largest term, which is regime 1 again.

    Nor is there anything to save. The eigh is not optional: applying \(e^{-\tau T}\) requires the spectral decomposition, and \(T\) is tridiagonal of dimension at most krylov_dim. Timed over an L = 8 run of 14 steps to \(\beta = 4\) with D reaching 128 — 2,925 propagator solves — all of _propagator_coeffs accounted for 0.49 % of a 37-second wall time, and the log-space assembly within it for 0.08 %. The average Krylov dimension was 5.3. Everything expensive is elsewhere: the matvecs at \(O(D^3)\) and the SVD splits.

    run() Cools In Place¶

    run() mutates the rho inside the Artifact it is handed. The stacked schedule wants exactly that — the artifact one phase returns is passed straight into the next — and it matches the rest of Alice, but it means an Artifact loaded from a checkpoint is consumed by the first run() it is given to. Branching two continuations off one checkpoint therefore needs the state reloaded, or copied, between them; a second run() on the same object starts from wherever the first one left it, and reports the \(\beta\) it was asked for instead of the \(\beta\) it reached.
